Most supplement and wellness brands aren’t trying to mislead anyone. They have research they believe in, and then the page gets written on a deadline and the wording drifts. Supports becomes fixes. May help becomes cures. Where the line sits lives in FTC guidance and a section of the Code of Federal Regulations that no marketing team has a reason to read.

I write The Substantiation File so that line is easier to see. Every Monday: what regulators did, what new studies found, and the sentence each one lets you say. The studies are linked, and so is the evidence for every claim line.

Why a neuroscientist

The FTC’s standard is competent and reliable scientific evidence. Whether a cell-culture study, an ingredient-level meta-analysis or a twelve-person open-label trial clears that bar is a scientific question, not a legal one.

I spent my research years at the bench. At Mount Sinai I worked in Andrew Varga’s lab, which studies how sleep and neurodegeneration interact, and wrote my master’s thesis on immune changes in a mouse model of tauopathy. I’m also a co-author on the lab’s 2026 paper in Scientific Reports on early-life sleep disruption in the same model. Before that I did undergraduate research in Anthony Fiumera’s lab at Binghamton. I was also nominated for the NSF I-Corps program, which trains researchers to take their work out of the lab. Outside academia I worked at Pandemic Response Lab, the testing lab Opentrons ran as a subsidiary, and at Saguaro Bloom Labs. Having been on the author side of peer review is why the newsletter reports a study’s design and sample before its headline.

The integrative neuroscience degree matters more than I expected on the wellness side. Rewires your brain. Boosts dopamine. Regulates your vagus nerve. Those are neuroscience claims, and checking them means reading the research rather than a summary of it.

What the newsletter checks against

FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance. The structure/function rules in 21 CFR 101.93. The FTC Endorsement Guides and the rule on fake reviews. Meta, Google and Amazon’s published policies. And for mechanism claims, the primary literature itself.

What it doesn’t do

Give legal advice, or tell any reader whether their own label or ad is compliant. The newsletter covers what evidence of a given kind supports and what the rules say about claims of that kind. Whether a specific product page holds up is a separate question, and the newsletter doesn’t answer it.

Not medical advice

We report what published research found. We do not recommend that anyone take, avoid or change any supplement, treatment or training program, and nothing we publish substitutes for advice from a qualified healthcare professional. The newsletter makes no health claims about any product.

What a research summary is and is not

Our summaries describe a study’s design, sample and reported results, based on the authors’ published abstract or article. A single study does not establish that an ingredient works, and a finding in one population, dose or formulation does not transfer to a different product. Read the linked source before relying on any figure.

What a claim line is and is not

Our “You can say / You cannot say” examples show how the evidence in one study maps to marketing language in general. They are not a review of anyone’s product. Whether a claim can lawfully be made depends on the formulation, dose, labeling and the full body of substantiation behind it. Supplement labels making structure/function claims still need the required FDA disclaimer and notification.

No guarantee

No review, ours or anyone’s, can guarantee that marketing will avoid a warning letter, an FTC action, a lawsuit, an ad rejection or an account restriction. Enforcement and platform moderation are discretionary. Our work reduces identified risk; it does not eliminate it.

Who does the work

ClaimFacts is written, and reviews are performed, by Haris Hillery, who holds an M.S. in Biomedical Science and Neuroscience from the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. That training is scientific, not legal. Our work compares marketing language against published FTC and FDA guidance and the scientific literature.

Dated content

Every issue and post reflects the research and regulations as of its publication date. Rules and guidance change, and older content is not updated unless we say so.

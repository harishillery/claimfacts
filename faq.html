FAQ

Questions we get asked.

Is this legal advice?

No, and we don't sell it as a substitute. We look at whether your evidence supports your claims and where your wording departs from published standards. When something turns on legal interpretation, we say so and tell you to take it to a lawyer. Nothing here creates an attorney-client relationship.

Will this make our copy boring?

That's the thing we work hardest to avoid. Every problem comes back with wording that keeps the promise. Most flagged lines end up stronger, because vague overstatement is usually standing in for a benefit nobody wrote properly in the first place.

Our agency already handles this.

Agencies get paid on performance, which is the incentive that produces the claims to begin with. We're not duplicating their work — we're the check their pay structure can't provide. Happy to work alongside them and send findings to both of you.

We already have a lawyer.

Then think of this as the pass that happens before their meter starts. Most of what we flag lives in ad captions, subject lines, and affiliate posts that never reach legal — not because anyone's careless, but because nobody sends forty Meta variants to a law firm.

We're a coaching business, not a supplement brand. Does this apply?

Yes, and often more sharply. The FTC's evidence standard attaches to health claims, not product types — a course, an app, and a capsule get treated the same way when each promises a change in how you feel or function. Wellness offers also carry two risks supplements don't: brain and nervous-system claims that outrun the research, and language that reads as clinical treatment.

Where's the line between coaching language and practicing therapy?

Roughly, where you name a diagnosable condition and promise to treat it. "Heals your anxiety," "resolves trauma," "for people with ADHD" — those read as clinical claims however the offer is structured, and licensing rules vary state by state. We'll flag it and explain what makes it read that way. Whether a specific phrase crosses your state's line is a legal question, and we'll point you to counsel rather than guess.

I'm a creator doing paid partnerships. Whose problem is the claim?

Both of yours. Under the FTC's endorsement rules, a claim you repeat becomes your claim, and the paid relationship has to be disclosed clearly. The upside is that clean claims and visible disclosure are exactly what get you approved by a brand's legal team — which is a real reason one creator gets picked over another with the same numbers.

Do you handle FDA warning letters?

Not as the lead. That's formal correspondence with a federal agency on a short clock, and it belongs with a regulatory attorney. We do the claim-language remediation underneath it and work alongside your lawyer. If you don't have one, we'll tell you to get one rather than take the job.

What counts as one piece?

One product page, one email, one ad and its copy, one label panel, one marketplace listing, one sales page, or one course module. A landing page and the ad pointing at it are two. Revisions to something we already reviewed that month don't count again.

What do you need to get started?

A link, or the copy pasted in. If you want us to check the evidence behind your claims, whatever studies or supplier documentation you're relying on. That's it — no call unless you want one.

What if we decide to ignore something you flag?

That's a legitimate business decision, and some brands make it knowingly on a top performer. The point of the report is that the decision is deliberate and written down rather than accidental.

Can you guarantee we won't get a warning letter?

No, and nobody can. Standards shift, platforms enforce inconsistently, and there's discretion involved. What review does is remove the language most likely to trigger action and give you a documented basis for the calls you made.

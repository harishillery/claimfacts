What we find

The same handful of lines, on almost every page.

Eleven phrases we run into constantly — six from supplement copy, five from wellness and mindset offers. What makes each one a problem, and the version that survives with the selling intact.

Supplements and functional foods

Clinically proven to reduce inflammation

FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance

"Clinically proven" is the highest bar there is. Research on the ingredient doesn't get you there — you're selling the finished product, and that's what needs the trial.

Try insteadMade with magnesium glycinate, studied for its role in normal muscle recovery

Helps with anxiety and depression

21 CFR 101.93(g) — disease claim

Both are diagnosable conditions. Naming them turns a supplement into an unapproved drug, and it doesn't matter which verb you put in front — helps, supports, and addresses all read the same way.

Try insteadSupports a calm, balanced mood during everyday stress

Doctor recommended

FTC substantiation — implied endorsement

This suggests a body of professional opinion. One physician on your advisory board doesn't back it up, and the FTC reads what a claim implies, not just what it literally says.

Try insteadFormulated with our medical advisor, Dr. [Name], [credentials]

It cured my chronic fatigue — verified buyer

16 CFR 255 — endorsement guides

A testimonial makes the claim yours. This one names a condition and describes an unusual result with nothing telling readers what typically happens.

Try insteadKeep the review, drop the condition, and put a typical-results note nearby

Boosts your immune system

FTC substantiation — implied prevention

"Boost" implies pushing above normal, which isn't what most immune research shows. Run it in flu season and it reads as a claim about preventing illness.

Try insteadProvides zinc and vitamin C, which contribute to normal immune function

FDA approved facility, GMP certified

Misstates regulatory status

The FDA doesn't approve supplements or the facilities that make them — it registers them. "Certified" points at a credential that doesn't exist in this form. This is the badge language most likely to read as deceptive.

Try insteadMade in an FDA-registered facility following cGMP requirements

Wellness programs, apps, and mindset offers

Rewire your brain in 21 days

FTC substantiation — mechanism and timeframe

Neuroplasticity is real. A 21-day rewiring timeline attached to your specific program is a performance claim, and the number is what makes it a claim rather than a hope.

Try insteadA 21-day practice built around how habits form

Heals anxiety and trauma at the root

Disease claim and scope of practice

Anxiety disorders and trauma are diagnosable, and "heals" is treatment language. This is the single most common phrase that moves a coaching offer into territory a licensing board would call clinical practice.

Try insteadSupport for navigating everyday stress and overwhelm, alongside clinical care rather than instead of it

Boosts dopamine and lowers cortisol naturally

FTC substantiation — unsupported mechanism

Neurochemical claims are the most common form of science-flavoured marketing here and among the least supported. Without biomarker data from your own program, you're describing a physiological effect you haven't measured.

Try insteadPractices associated with feeling calmer and more focused, drawn from stress-regulation research

My client came off her medication after 8 weeks

16 CFR 255 — atypical result, treatment substitution

A testimonial makes the claim yours, and this one implies your program replaces prescribed treatment. It's the riskiest sentence a wellness coach can publish, because if someone acts on it the harm is immediate rather than financial.

Try insteadCut it. There's no rewrite that makes a medication-discontinuation story safe to publish.

Backed by neuroscience

FTC substantiation — implied product-level evidence

Research on a mechanism isn't research on your program. This invites people to believe your specific offer has been studied — the same gap as ingredient research standing in for product research in supplements.

Try insteadBuilt on published research into [specific mechanism], with the studies cited on the page

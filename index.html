Every study ends with a Claim Line format illustration

An eight-week randomized trial in healthy adults finds a modest improvement in how quickly participants fall asleep. Small sample, one formulation, one dose.

You can sayContains an ingredient studied for its role in supporting restful sleep.

You cannot sayClinically proven to fix insomnia.

Illustrative, not a real study. The real ones come with the paper, the numbers, and the authors' own limitation sentence.

What's in each issue

1

The Regulatory Desk

New FTC actions, FDA warning letters, and self-regulatory decisions from the past week. Three warning-letter classifications with their citations and what triggers them, rotated weekly. And one standing rule that people keep getting wrong. If nothing landed, we say so in a sentence rather than inventing news.

2

Five or six new studies

Supplements and ingredients, training, brain and nervous system, psychology. Design named up front, sample size stated, the real numbers quoted. At least one null result most weeks, because a trial that found nothing is the most useful thing to read before you build a campaign on that ingredient.

3

The Claim Line, under every study

What the evidence lets you put on a label or in an ad, written as copy you can use, next to the version that would get you flagged. When the evidence supports nothing marketable, we say that plainly.

4

The Takeaway

One paragraph, one idea: the single thing that should change what you write this week.

Who reads it

Brand operators

Founders and marketers at supplement and wellness brands who write the product pages and ads, and who are the ones holding the rejection notice when a platform disagrees.

Creators doing paid partnerships

A claim you repeat in a sponsored post becomes your claim. This is how you know which ones you can repeat.

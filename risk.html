Why it matters

The bill almost never arrives as a fine.

Regulators acting on a small brand is the rare outcome. The common ones are faster, quieter, and land on whichever part of your business you can least afford to pause.

Your ads stop running

Meta and Google reject health claims automatically, and repeat flags escalate to the account itself. Appeals take weeks. Every day the account is down, your acquisition is zero and your costs aren't.

Your listings vanish

Amazon suppresses supplement listings with disease claims, usually without warning and usually mid sales cycle. Getting reinstated needs documentation nobody assembles in advance.

The claim changes what you're selling

Say your product treats or prevents a disease and it is legally an unapproved drug. That's the thread FDA warning letters pull, and the one plaintiffs' firms read those letters looking for.

Coaching starts to look like treatment

If you sell programs, the FTC isn't your only exposure. Promising to heal a named condition can read as clinical practice to a state licensing board — and that complaint usually comes from a competitor or an unhappy client, not a regulator.

Why one cleanup isn't enough

Fixing what's live today fixes today. Next week someone on your team writes four new ad variants on a deadline. The month after, a new product ships with new packaging. An affiliate posts something nobody looked at. Your agency rewrites the landing page for Q4.

Every one of those puts the problem back, because the person writing is optimizing for click-through and has never read an FTC guidance document. That's the argument for ongoing review — and the reason we treat a one-time audit as a starting point rather than a solution.
